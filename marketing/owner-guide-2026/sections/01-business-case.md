# The Business Case

## Why this exists

You already run a CRM. That part is not new. What is new is that the crew now logs in too. Marcus, Aisha, Diego, Patrick, and the subs who walk on and off your jobs every week now have their own door into the same system you and Brenda already use. They walk onto a Buckhead repaint, open the portal on their phone, see the job, see the punch list, take five photos, and clock in. By the time they pull out of the driveway that afternoon, the office already has the day's record. Nothing waits until Friday. Nothing lives in a screenshot.

For years the field-to-office handoff has run on text messages, group chats, screenshots forwarded twice, and the end-of-week conversation where Brenda chases down hours and Patrick tries to remember which Brookhaven job was finished Tuesday and which was finished Wednesday. That worked when Bridgepointe was three guys and a truck. It does not scale to ten employees, six to eight active subs, and a project pipeline averaging $145K a job. The portal puts photos, time, status, and pay communication into one place that follows every job from kickoff to final invoice, in whichever language each crew member prefers. It is not a fancier phone tree. It is the system of record.

The shift you are making is small in technical terms and large in operational terms. The CRM at `/admin` keeps doing what it has always done — that is your view, and Brenda's, and Patrick's. The portal at `/portal` is the new piece. It is a door, sized to a phone, that lets the field push information *in* instead of waiting for the office to pull it *out*. Once that door is open and the crew is using it daily, the office stops being the bottleneck. That is the entire point.

## The five problems this solves

### 1. Photos in one place, not 47 group texts

Right now, before-and-after photos for a master bath remodel in Brookhaven live on Marcus's iPhone, on Aisha's Android, on Diego's cracked screen that he has been meaning to replace since February, and in a WhatsApp thread with three subs that nobody scrolls back through. When a homeowner disputes whether the trim was sanded before the second coat, you have to text three people and hope. When a phone breaks or gets sold, those photos are gone.

The portal stores every photo by project, by phase, with before/during/after tags. Anyone in the office can pull up the full visual history of a job in five seconds. Based on 240 jobs a year at an average $145K project, even one disputed phase per quarter that gets resolved by pulling up a timestamped photo saves $2,000 to $4,000 a year in argued change orders and avoided rework. That is before you count the marketing photos that no longer get lost when somebody upgrades to a new phone.

### 2. Crew clocks themselves in

Right now, Brenda spends most of Friday morning chasing timesheets. She texts Marcus. She calls Diego. She squints at a hand-written sheet from a sub that has Tuesday's hours written over Monday's. By the time payroll is reconciled, half the day is gone. Four hours a week, fifty weeks a year, on a $58,000 base salary at office-overhead loaded rate, that is roughly $5,500 a year of office time spent on a problem the crew can solve in ten seconds by tapping a button at the start and end of their shift.

The portal lets every in-house employee clock in and out from their phone at the job site. The hours flow straight into the office view. Brenda's job changes from chasing to reviewing and approving — which takes thirty minutes, not four hours. As a bonus, you stop losing money to rounding-up errors. When a hand-written sheet says "got there around 8," that almost never means 8:14. The portal records the actual minute.

### 3. Subs get clarity on what they owe and what they're owed

Your subcontractors call the office. A lot. "Did you guys pay invoice 0142?" "When does my next payment hit?" "What did we agree on for the Buckhead trim job?" Every one of those calls pulls Brenda or Patrick away from work that actually moves jobs forward. One to two calls a week per active sub, across six to eight subs, runs about an hour of office time a week answering questions the sub could answer himself. At the same loaded office rate, that is roughly $2,500 a year recovered.

The portal gives each sub a billings dashboard. They log in, they see what is open, what is paid, what is scheduled. They stop calling, or they call only when something is actually wrong, which is what calls should be for.

### 4. Status from the field, in real time

When Marcus marks the prime coat complete on a Buckhead exterior repaint, the office knows immediately. Patrick does not have to wait for the Friday roundup to find out a phase wrapped on Tuesday. Brenda does not have to discover, three weeks later, that a billable phase was finished on the 14th and never invoiced. That kind of lag is normal in this business and it is not free. Cash that should have been collected on day 30 gets collected on day 38 instead, and you are essentially financing your client's project for an extra week.

On $1.5M in annual revenue, shaving five to ten days off the average billing cycle saves around $1,500 a year just in financing cost on the float — and that is a conservative estimate at current short-term rates. The bigger win is psychological. You stop having the conversation that starts with "I thought we billed for that already."

### 5. Bilingual by default

Roughly half of the trade workforce on Atlanta residential remodels speaks Spanish at home. Forcing a crew member to navigate English-only software adds friction on every interaction. They squint, they guess, they ask the foreman, they sometimes just stop using the tool. Multiply that by every clock-in, every photo upload, every status update, every week, and you have built a tax on your own crew's time.

The portal toggles English and Spanish per user, set once at the profile level. Diego sees Spanish. Patrick sees English. Same data, same job, same workflow. Onboarding a new Spanish-first crew member takes minutes instead of a week of supervised use. The number of "I didn't understand the form" mistakes drops to near zero.

## The math, conservatively

| Line item | Annual upside |
|---|---|
| Photo organization (resolved disputes + retained marketing photos) | $3,000 |
| Bookkeeper time recovered (timesheet chase + sub questions) | $5,500 |
| Faster billing (5-day cash improvement on $1.5M revenue) | $1,500 |
| Subs answering their own billing questions | $2,500 |
| Reduced rework from early issue flagging (one prevented re-paint) | $4,000 |
| Marketing content from auto-photographed jobs (social + portfolio) | $5,000 |
| **Total annual upside** | **~$21,500** |

This is conservative. It does not count the harder-to-price wins: fewer awkward conversations with clients about disputed work, less weekend stress on Patrick, faster turnaround on referrals because every job has a photographable result, and the simple fact that crew members who feel like the office sees their work tend to stay longer. Painter and crew turnover in Atlanta residential remodeling runs hot every spring. If the portal is part of why one good lead painter sticks around an extra year, you have just saved a recruiting and onboarding cost that easily clears five thousand dollars on its own.

The other thing the table does not show is leverage. Most of these line items get bigger as Bridgepointe grows. If you go from $1.5M to $2M in revenue, the billing-cycle savings grow. If you take on two more subs, the dashboard hours saved grow. The portal does not get more expensive when the company grows. The benefit does.

> This pays for itself the first time it prevents one disputed phase from getting re-done.

## What it doesn't do

Be clear with yourself and with your managers about the boundaries. The portal is not a replacement for QuickBooks. You still need QuickBooks for taxes, for payroll runs, for the accountant. The portal feeds clean hours and clean billing data into that workflow — it does not replace it.

The portal is also not a scheduling or dispatch tool. Patrick still owns the calendar. The portal will tell you who clocked in on what job, but it is not where you decide who goes where on Tuesday morning. And it is not a direct-message system. Conversation still belongs on the phone, on Slack, on WhatsApp — wherever your team already talks. The portal is for *records*. It is the place you go when somebody asks "what actually happened?" not the place you go to ask the crew a question.

What the portal IS: the single source of truth for who worked where, when, with what photos, with what notes, and what got paid. If it is not in the portal, treat it as if it did not happen. That rule, enforced consistently for ninety days, is what turns the portal from a piece of software into the way Bridgepointe operates.

## What success looks like in 90 days

By the end of the first quarter of real use, you should be able to walk into your office on a Friday morning and see all of these:

- 100% of in-house employees — Marcus, Aisha, Diego, Patrick, and the rest — are clocking in via the portal. Paper timesheets are gone.
- Every active project has at least five progress photos uploaded, tagged by phase.
- Subs are checking their billings dashboard before they call the office. The "did you pay invoice 0142?" call is rare, not weekly.
- Brenda's Friday timesheet routine has shrunk from a four-hour chase to a thirty-minute review-and-approve.
- At least one disputed change order — the kind that used to turn into a long phone call with a Brookhaven homeowner — has been resolved cleanly by pulling up the portal's photo timeline.

If you hit those five marks by month three, the portal is doing exactly what it was built to do. If you are not hitting them, the answer is almost never that the system is broken. It is that one of the managers — usually the office manager or the lead foreman — has not been briefed firmly enough on the expectation that this is now how Bridgepointe works. That is the conversation worth having before the next quarter starts.
